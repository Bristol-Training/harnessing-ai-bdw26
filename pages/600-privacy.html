Responsible Use — Ethics, Privacy & Fairness

Key Takeaways
  • Transparency, human oversight, privacy, and fairness are the UNESCO principles most directly relevant to everyday AI use
  • You remain responsible for every output you act on or share, regardless of whether AI generated it
  • Never submit other people’s personal data to a commercial AI tool without checking the tool’s privacy policy and your right to share it
  • AI systems inherit societal biases — actively check outputs for bias rather than assuming they are neutral
  • Intellectual property questions around AI-generated content are unresolved — treat AI output as a draft requiring genuine human authorship

Transparency

The UNESCO Recommendation places transparency at the heart of responsible AI use. Being transparent means acknowledging when AI has contributed to your work — whether you are drafting a document, writing a social media post, or preparing a report. Transparency is not about apologising for using AI; it is about honesty with your audience.

The level of disclosure expected varies by context. A personal message drafted with AI assistance carries different obligations from a published opinion piece or a report submitted to a client. In professional, academic, and journalistic contexts, disclosure is increasingly expected as a baseline standard. When in doubt, disclose.

Human Oversight

UNESCO is explicit that humans must remain in control of decisions that affect people’s lives. AI must not be the final decision-maker in employment, healthcare, legal proceedings, education, or any other high-stakes context. This principle also operates at the individual level: you are responsible for any output you act on or share, even when AI generated it.

Human oversight in practice

❌ Sending an AI-drafted message to a vulnerable person without reading it carefully
❌ Acting on medical, legal, or financial information from an LLM without consulting a qualified professional
❌ Sharing AI-generated statistics without verifying them from primary sources
✅ Using AI to draft, then reviewing carefully before sending
✅ Using AI to generate options, then applying your own judgement
✅ Treating AI output as a starting point, never a finished product

Privacy and Data Protection

When you type a prompt into a commercial AI tool, your text travels to the provider’s servers, is processed by systems you do not control, and may be stored, used for model training, reviewed by human contractors, or subject to legal jurisdictions different from your own. The exact treatment depends on the tool and the account tier.

What not to submit to a commercial AI tool

🚫 Other people’s personal information — names, addresses, health data, financial details
🚫 Information shared with you in confidence — medical, legal, or personal disclosures
🚫 Unpublished work or commercially sensitive material
🚫 Passwords, API keys, or security credentials
🚫 Any content you would not be comfortable seeing in a data breach

Different categories of information carry different levels of risk:

Category Risk Examples
Public information 🟢 Low Published facts, general knowledge, public news
Anonymised information 🟡 Medium De-identified data — re-identification is sometimes possible
Personal information 🔴 High Names, locations, health, financial details
Third-party personal data 🔴 High Information about others who have not consented
Sensitive personal data 🔴 Very high Health, religion, ethnicity, sexual orientation, criminal records

When choosing an AI tool for tasks involving sensitive information, look for a clear and readable privacy policy, the ability to opt out of training data collection, and compliance with data protection law in your country (such as GDPR in the UK and EU). For learning and general use, privacy-respecting tools such as duck.ai provide a good default — DuckDuckGo does not save conversations or use inputs for model training.

Fairness and Non-Discrimination

AI systems are trained on data that reflects the inequalities and prejudices present in society. They can reproduce and amplify those biases in ways that are subtle and difficult to detect — particularly because the outputs look fluent and authoritative.

Common patterns to watch for include gender bias (associating professions or leadership with particular genders), racial and ethnic bias (stereotypical associations or underrepresentation of certain groups), socioeconomic bias (assuming access to resources or education), and geographic bias (centring Western, English-speaking perspectives as the default).

Bias detection questions

When reviewing any AI output, ask:

  • Representation: Who is included in the examples, and who is absent?
  • Language: Are descriptions fair and respectful to all groups?
  • Assumptions: What unstated assumptions are embedded in the response?
  • Perspectives: Whose viewpoint is centred, and whose is absent?
  • Stereotypes: Are any harmful generalisations present?

Producing more equitable outputs requires deliberate prompting — explicitly requesting diverse examples, asking for multiple perspectives, and challenging responses that default to a narrow worldview. Even then, the model’s knowledge of underrepresented groups is often shallower and less reliable, making independent verification more important, not less.

Intellectual Property

LLMs are trained on vast quantities of text collected from the web, including books, journalism, and academic papers — much of it copyrighted, and much of it gathered without explicit consent from the authors. This is actively contested in courts in several countries.

Three practical issues arise for users. First, under current UK law, a work created entirely by AI without human authorship may not attract copyright protection, meaning you may not own what the AI generates for you. Second, submitting AI-generated work as entirely your own in professional or academic contexts may constitute misrepresentation. Third, AI outputs may contain phrases closely derived from copyrighted source material in ways that are invisible to you.

The most reliable approach is to treat AI outputs as drafts requiring genuine human authorship: revise substantively, add your own analysis and judgement, and attribute AI assistance where your context requires it.

The Human Dimensions of AI

Beyond the formal principles, responsible use also involves asking what is lost — not just what is gained — when AI takes over a task.

LLM training relies on human labellers who evaluate outputs and identify harmful content, work known as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This labour is frequently outsourced to workers in lower-income countries, often under precarious conditions. The benefits of AI accrue largely to wealthy technology companies and their users; the costs are disproportionately borne by others.

There is also a question about authenticity in human communication. Using AI to generate a response to a heartfelt message from a colleague or friend may signal — consciously or not — that their communication did not warrant genuine attention. And in learning and professional contexts, the process of writing and thinking has value that is separate from the quality of the finished product. Delegating that process to AI can produce an acceptable document while eroding the expertise that comes from doing the work yourself.

These are not reasons to avoid AI. They are reasons to choose deliberately.
