Hallucinations — Hands-on

Key Takeaways
  • Hallucinations are confident-sounding fabrications: the model generates plausible text that is factually wrong
  • Hallucinations are not “bugs” — they are a direct consequence of how LLMs work (pattern completion, not fact retrieval)
  • Certain types of prompts are more likely to trigger hallucinations than others
  • Learning to provoke hallucinations in a controlled setting is one of the best ways to develop critical instincts about AI output

What is a Hallucination?

An LLM does not retrieve facts from a database the way a search engine retrieves web pages. Instead, it predicts the most statistically likely continuation of your input based on patterns in its training data. This means that when you ask for something very specific—a date, a citation, a name, a statistic—the model may produce a confident-sounding answer that is simply invented.

This is called a hallucination: output that is fluent, plausible, and wrong.

Hallucinations are most likely when:

  • You ask for specific facts the model may not have been trained on (obscure events, niche statistics, recent information)
  • You ask for citations, DOIs, or URLs — models frequently invent these
  • You ask about real people’s specific actions or quotes
  • You ask about your own life or local events the model has no data about
  • You ask for numerical data — dates, percentages, prices, counts
Why hallucinations are dangerous

The problem is not that LLMs make mistakes — so do humans. The problem is that hallucinations often sound exactly like correct answers. The model’s tone and formatting do not change when it is making something up. It may even cite a plausible-sounding paper that does not exist.

🦆 Exercise A: The Fake Citation

Open duck.ai and enter:

Give me three academic references (author, year, journal, title) about the effects of urban green spaces on mental health

Read the references carefully, then verify them:

  • Search for each paper in Google Scholar or PubMed
  • Do the papers exist? Do the authors, journals, and titles match?
What to expect

You are very likely to find that at least one or two of the references do not exist as cited. The journal may be real, the topic may be real, but the specific paper—with that title, those authors, and that year—was invented.

This is one of the most dangerous hallucinations in professional and academic contexts, because:

  • The format looks completely legitimate
  • Readers (and the author) may not check
  • Fake citations can undermine the credibility of otherwise sound work

What to do: Never include an AI-generated citation in any work without independently verifying that the paper exists and says what you think it says.

🦆 Exercise B: The Overconfident Statistic

Open duck.ai and enter:

What percentage of people in the UK are vegetarian? Give me the most recent figure available.

Then try variations:

What percentage of people in [your country] are vegetarian?

How many people in the world are vegetarian?

What to expect

The model will likely give you a confident-sounding percentage. It may cite a survey or organisation. Now:

  1. Search for the actual figure using a search engine
  2. Check whether the number the model gave is in the right ballpark—or plausible but wrong—or completely fabricated
  3. Notice whether the model hedged (“approximately”, “according to some surveys”) or stated it as a definite fact

Key insight: Percentages, counts, and statistics are high-risk territory for hallucinations, especially when the question involves recent data. Always go to primary sources (official surveys, ONS, Eurostat, etc.) for numbers you intend to use professionally.

🦆 Exercise C: The Invented Event

Open duck.ai and ask about something very specific and obscure:

What happened at the 1987 International Symposium on Freshwater Ecology in Lausanne?

Or invent your own obscure question:

Tell me about the 2003 Scandinavian Conference on Workplace Wellbeing

What to expect

The model may:

  • Confidently describe an event that never existed
  • Describe a plausible-sounding programme of speakers and sessions
  • Hedge appropriately and say it has no information

The interesting outcome to watch for is confident fabrication: the model invents a plausible event and presents it as fact. This is most likely when the subject is obscure enough that the model has little or no relevant training data, so it fills the gap with pattern-matched plausibility.

When the model does hedge well (“I don’t have reliable information about that specific event”), that is the correct, honest behaviour. Notice the contrast.

🦆 Exercise D: Provoking a Hallucination About You

Open duck.ai and ask:

What awards has [your name] won?

Or:

What research has [a friend or family member’s name] published?

Use the name of a real person who is not famous and would not appear in training data.

What to expect

The model is likely to either:

  • Appropriately say it has no information about this person, or
  • Invent plausible-sounding accomplishments, publications, or awards

If it invents something, notice how real it sounds. This illustrates exactly the risk when AI is used to research people: the output may be completely fabricated but convincingly formatted.

This exercise also has a privacy implication: if your name does appear in training data (because you have publications, news coverage, or a public profile), the model may pull in real information—but may also mix it with errors or confuse you with someone else with the same name.

🦆 Exercise E: Asking the Model to Reflect

After completing the exercises above, try asking the model directly:

In the responses you just gave me, which parts were you most uncertain about? Where might you have made something up?

What to observe

This is a fascinating meta-exercise. The model may:

  • Give a thoughtful, honest reflection on the limits of its knowledge
  • Identify the high-risk areas it covered (statistics, citations, obscure events)
  • Hedge appropriately

However, it may also misidentify which parts were risky or fail to flag fabrications it made. This shows that self-reflection in LLMs is imperfect — the model cannot reliably detect its own hallucinations.

Key takeaway: You cannot ask an LLM to tell you if it hallucinated. External verification is the only reliable check.

Summary: Reducing Hallucination Risk

Situation Risk level What to do
Asking for factual statistics or percentages 🔴 High Verify with official sources
Asking for citations or DOIs 🔴 High Always verify in Google Scholar / PubMed
Asking about obscure events or people 🔴 High Treat output as potentially fabricated
Asking for recent information 🟡 Medium Check the model’s training cut-off; use a tool with web access
Asking for well-established general concepts 🟢 Lower Still worth checking nuanced claims
Asking for creative writing or brainstorming 🟢 Lower Factual accuracy not the main concern
The golden rule

Fluency is not evidence of accuracy. A well-formatted, confident-sounding answer may be completely wrong. Always ask: “What would I need to verify before acting on this?”
